# First counts stall at setup, not scanning

The owner is right that a new bar can find 86'd complicated, but the complication is not where he thinks. Snapping a label and typing a count is short, it works the way people expect, and it is barely explained. A new user reaches a counted bottle in about **7 in-app taps with Sign in with Apple**, or 13 taps and about 62 typed characters by email. On the way they read a 51-word start card, which is within every benchmark for short guidance, and they aim a shutter at a label the way Google Lens and the iOS Notes scanner taught them. The friction comes after the scan. On a first count, Review & Par shows every bottle as red "Not set" and "Unassigned", and the order stays blocked until each bottle has two things: a par, set with a stepper that adds one per tap (about **145 taps for 20 bottles at par 6**), and a distributor with an order email. On one path the app gives no way to add that distributor at all. Meanwhile the first App Store screenshot promises the order splits by distributor "on its own. No organizing required." The website promises "15 minutes" and "No setup day", and the sales pitch says "Just download and go." Two smaller problems make it worse. A fresh install opens on a "Welcome back" sign-in screen with the trial button below the fold. And after the founder's warm sales call, a new bar hears nothing until a payment reminder around day 10. Most of the fixes are copy or small changes in one build: say plainly that the first count sets each bottle up once, let people type a par and stop coloring it as an error, close the dead ends, and have the founder hand new bars off in person. One fix is needed for App Review: on first run, a custom "Enable Camera / Not now" screen sits under the iOS camera alert, the same pre-prompt layout an Apple reviewer rejected in March 2026. At about five sign-ups a week, no A/B test can judge any of these changes. Per-user timelines built from data the server already records, a few new in-app events, and watching real first counts can.

## The scan is the easy part: seven taps to a counted bottle

The owner's worry has four parts. Each was checked against the research evidence and against what the app does today: the code, the rendered screens in `research_notes/86d first run onboarding/screens/`, the App Store listing, the website, the sales pitch, and the emails the backend sends. Three of the four point at the wrong stage of the first run.

| The worry | Verdict | What the app and the evidence show |
|---|---|---|
| Too complicated to get started | **Wrong for the first bottle, right for the first order** | 7 in-app taps (Apple) or 13 taps + ~62 characters (email) to a counted bottle; nothing about the bar is asked before the camera. The heavy part is Review & Order: a par and a distributor email for every bottle |
| "They're used to snapping a picture of the label" | **Mostly wrong about the snap, right about what surrounds it** | The shutter-on-a-label flow is the convention people know. The mismatches are in the claims around it ("no typing", "instantly", "on its own") and in competitors' fill-level and whole-shelf promises |
| Not clear enough | **Right, in specific places** | Instruction text below WCAG contrast minimums, a first-scan status line clipped off screen, Review painted in error red, truncated chips, dead-end messages, no help anywhere in the app |
| Too much information up front that people click through | **Wrong about the amount, right about the repetition** | One 51-word card, already cut from a carousel. But it is a mandatory tap before every count, and it repeats the first-run tips and an untrue camera line every week. That teaches people to click through |

**Getting to the first bottle is short.** On the email path the screens are Login, Register (four fields plus a terms checkbox), "What's your bar called?" (skippable), the start card, the iOS camera alert, the viewfinder and the number pad. The Apple path replaces Register with one tap and Apple's own sheet. The app asks for no location, staff name, par, price or distributor before the first scan; a location called "My Bar" is created silently (`src/context/LocationContext.tsx:61-67`). Before the camera, an email sign-up sees about **198 words of app copy across four screens, and an Apple sign-up about 139**. Over half of those words are sign-in labels and legal lines, not product explanation. This is heavier than camera-first system tools like Apple's Visual Intelligence and the Notes scanner, which need no account. But it is far lighter than 86'd's own category. Backbar's help center calls uploading and matching the product spreadsheet "the most important step and also the most time consuming" ([Backbar](https://www.getbackbar.com/support/introduction)). WISK offers no free trial and charges an implementation fee from $750 that covers "catalog setup, and first-inventory walkthrough" ([WISK](https://www.wisk.ai/price)). One Backbar reviewer wrote: "It took about a day to fully set up all my items and do my first full inventory" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/backbar/id1461796151?see-all=reviews&platform=ipad)). 86'd builds the bar's product list as a side effect of scanning. Competitors charge for that, or make it a project.

**There is not too much information up front.** The owner's instinct matches the evidence, but the app already acted on it. In NN/g's controlled test of mobile tutorials (70 users, 4 iPhone apps), people who read the tutorial were no more successful than people who skipped it (91% vs 94%, not significant), and they rated the tasks harder (4.92 vs 5.49 on a 7-point scale, p = 0.047) ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-tutorials/)). A CHI 2012 field experiment with more than 45,000 players found tutorials paid off only in the most complex, least conventional game; in the simpler games the effect was "surprisingly negligible" ([Andersen et al.](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)). In a vendor A/B test with more than 160,000 users, Vevo removed its tutorial and saw logins rise almost 10% and sign-ups about 6% ([Mobile World Live](https://www.mobileworldlive.com/focus-apps/apptimize-onboarding-tutorials-value-ab-testing/)). 86'd removed its post-sign-up pitch screen in #26 for exactly these reasons. What is left is one card: a headline, one sentence, three one-line tips, a camera note and one button. People read half the words only on pages of **111 words or fewer** ([Nielsen](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/)). Chameleon's tour data favours about two lines per step and finds completion halves between four steps (46%) and five (23%) ([Chameleon 2019](https://www.chameleon.io/assets/chameleon-product-tour-benchmarks-report-2019.pdf)). The card is inside both limits.

The click-through the owner fears does happen, but to returning users. `showStartScreen` starts as `true` on every camera mount (`src/screens/CameraScan.tsx:109`), and the tips version is chosen only by `bottleCount === 0`. A fully sent order empties the draft. So the first-run tips, including "We'll ask for camera access next", come back at the start of every weekly count, long after access was granted (`CameraScan.tsx:944-982`). The project notes describe this screen as shown "only when nothing has been counted yet". That is true of the current draft, not of the user. Repeating a static message is how people learn to ignore it. In a three-week mobile field study, adherence to repeated permission warnings fell to about 55% when the warning looked the same each time, against nearly 80% when its appearance varied ([Temple Fox](https://www3.fox.temple.edu/discover/?p=312397)). Material's guidance is blunt: "Don't show it to returning users" ([Material](https://m1.material.io/growth-communications/onboarding.html)). The card also renders its most important sentence the faintest. The camera note is 12 pt `#737373` on `#1A1A1A`, a **3.67:1 contrast ratio, below WCAG AA's 4.5:1** ([screenshot](<../research_notes/86d first run onboarding/screens/01-start-iphone16.png>)). The code comment calls that sentence "the cheapest protection the permission has".

**"Snap a picture of the label" is what the app already does.** Tapping a shutter at one label matches Google Lens on iOS and the Notes scanner's manual mode. On a crowded back bar it is safer than auto-capture: one Scandit user complained auto-capture is "so fast that sometimes you can't 'aim'" and grabs "the first available code" ([G2](https://g2.com/products/scandit-barcode-scanning/reviews/scandit-barcode-scanning-review-10774139)). The dimmed edges of the viewfinder show exactly what the AI receives, the same idea as VisionKit's region of interest. The listing's own scan screenshot says "Snap a bottle. AI names it." The start card's three tips ("Point camera at the bottle label", "Tap the shutter — AI identifies the bottle", "Type your total stock count on the number pad") describe what the camera then asks. So the gesture is not the mismatch.

**The mismatches are in the claims around the snap.** Before install, screenshot 4 says "No barcodes, no typing. You just tap the count." But the description says "You just type the count", and the camera has a barcode button. Screenshot 3 shows on-hand counts of 3.5 and 4.75 with nothing saying they were typed by hand ([listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/86d-bar-inventory/id6798359825); [screenshot 3](<../research_notes/86d first run onboarding/screens/appstore/listing-position-3_86d-2-Par-1206x2622.png>)). Prospects also arrive primed by competitors. BarOptix says it "records every open bottle to the tenth" ([BarOptix](https://www.baroptix.com/)). Home-bar apps claim one photo identifies "up to 10 bottles at once" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6759352854)). One Partender reviewer believed that app "uses image recognition to measure bottles", though it is tap-the-level ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/partender-bar-inventory/id726829071?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone)). Operators say they are comfortable with AI (87% in Toast's 2026 survey of 676 operators, [Toast](https://pos.toasttab.com/blog/data/voice-of-the-restaurant-industry-2026)), but only 26% use AI tools ([Restaurant Dive](https://restaurantdive.com/news/national-restaurant-assocation-operator-artificial-intelligence-adoption/812418)). Expect curiosity, not fluency. Google's PAIR guidebook says to "Be up-front about what your product can and can't do the first time" and to avoid promising "AI magic" ([PAIR](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/mental-models/)). The app does the first: the card says you type the count. Two things undercut it. The card says "AI identifies each bottle instantly", but the pad is built for identification to finish late, with a 25-second watchdog. And the empty distributor sheet says "You're one step away from magic ✨".

There are mismatches after the snap too. On the very first scan, the hint bubble takes the room needed for the "Identifying in the background" status line, so it is clipped off screen. The header keeps saying "Scanning" after the bottle has been named ([screenshot](<../research_notes/86d first run onboarding/screens/05a-pad-identifying-iphone16.png>)). The white corner brackets frame about 46% × 36% of the picture, while the AI receives about 84% × 87% ([screenshot](<../research_notes/86d first run onboarding/screens/04-camera-iphone16.png>); `src/utils/scanImage.ts:37-50`). Nothing shows what a good shot looks like until a failure says "move closer". Nothing says how to count an open bottle, although the pad has a "." key and the order math treats stock as full bottles plus a fraction (`src/utils/orderQuantity.ts:7-8`). NN/g's study of camera-based apps found that specific "how to hold and aim" guidance prevents failures ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ar-walkthroughs/)). Help delivered "as closely as possible to when they were needed" raised play time 16% and progress 40% in the one game where any help mattered ([Andersen et al.](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)). The skill worth teaching is framing the bottle, and the place to teach it is the live viewfinder, not a card read 20 seconds earlier.

**"Not clear enough" is right, and the measurements show where.** One grey token, `#737373`, carries most instructions in the scan flow. On the app's near-black surfaces it measures 3.7–4.4:1, below AA: "Tap the shutter to scan" is 4.37:1, and "BOTTLES ON HAND" and "Identifying in the background" are 4.04:1. The amber "The second AI read X — check the label" line is 10 pt, and the red "Add Bottle" button under it does not change. In Review, the amber chip's instruction "Tap if this row is right" is cut off by an ellipsis ([screenshot](<../research_notes/86d first run onboarding/screens/09-review-iphone16.png>)). "Couldn't recognize — add it manually" offers only "Retake Photo", and adding by name lives behind Review's "+". There is no help item anywhere in the app. The App Store's Support link opens the privacy policy, and the unlinked `/support` page describes screens that no longer exist.

## The first order is a setup day nobody announced

The friction the owner senses is on the second screen of the count, not the first. When the bartender taps Done, Review & Par shows every bottle under "UNASSIGNED · Tap item to assign". Each has a red PAR stepper stuck at 1, a red "Not set" badge and a red "Assign →" chip. The footer reads "2 ITEMS MISSING A PAR LEVEL" in red ([screenshot](<../research_notes/86d first run onboarding/screens/09-review-iphone16.png>)). Red does four jobs here (brand, buttons, links, warnings), so a first count that has gone perfectly looks like a page of errors. "Generate Order Summary" is a hard stop: "Set par levels first — 2 items still have the default par level (1)…" (`src/screens/ReviewGrid.tsx:135-154`). Par cannot be typed in Review. The on-hand number can be tapped and typed, but par only moves +1 per tap from 1, with no long-press repeat (`ReviewGrid.tsx:160-162, 722-746`). Each bottle then needs a distributor, which means typing its name and order email in a sheet. The keyboard's "next" key on the name field does not move to the email field (`ReviewGrid.tsx:382-390`). The code audit's arithmetic for N bottles at par P is about N·(P−1) + 2·(N−1) + 7 taps, plus 30–45 characters. For **20 bottles at par 6 that is about 145 taps**, the most expensive part of the first run by far.

Several traps wait on the way to the first order. If someone reaches Order Summary with no distributor saved, the assign sheet says "No distributors added yet." with no button and no link. The only way back is the sidebar (`src/screens/OrderSummary.tsx:653-736`). A bottle is ordered only when stock is below 70% of par (`src/utils/orderQuantity.ts:1-29`). So a bartender who sets each par equal to what's on the shelf, a natural first guess, gets "All Stocked!" and has nothing to send. The only explanation is a footer that credits a fixed arithmetic rule to "AI" ("AI reorders a bottle below 0.7 of its par"). If the bar name was skipped, a modal asks for it at the moment of sending, which is the timing #34 set out to avoid. The default location then shows up in the distributor's email as "Order sent by Dana Reyes, Bar Manager at The Corner Tavern (My Bar)", because the sign-off adds the location in brackets whenever it differs from the bar name (`86d-api/helpers.py:766-789`).

None of this is announced anywhere a prospect forms expectations. **The listing's first screenshot, which is what search results show, says "AI organizes the order and splits it by distributor on its own. No organizing required."** Its second says "AI sends the order", but the email is a fixed template. The scan screenshot is fourth of four. Apple shows only the first one to three screenshots in search results ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/product-page/)), so a shopper never sees the step the first run begins with. The file names number the shots 1-Scan, 2-Par, 3-Order, 4-SendEmail, and the listing shows them 3, 4, 2, 1, which looks like an upload slip ([iTunes lookup](https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?id=6798359825&country=us)). The website leads with "Count the bar. Send every order. 15 minutes." and "No setup day: the first count builds the bottle book" ([my86d.com](https://my86d.com)). The login screen says "AI Bar inventory in 10 minutes — not hours." The founder's reference email says "Just download and go" (`86d-api/pitch.py:119-140`). The only honest hint is screenshot 3's caption, "after your first order". Each of these claims is true of the second count. Not one says the first count is where each bottle gets a par and a distributor.

This matters more for a bar than for a consumer app because of who is counting and when. Counts are usually done by a manager or owner-operator, late after close or before opening, in storerooms with poor signal ([Backbar](https://www.getbackbar.com/how-to-do-bar-inventory); [BevSpot](https://bevspot.com/blog/6-ways-make-bar-inventory-faster/)). In Backbar's poll of 11,240 bar and restaurant workers, **52% count weekly and 26% monthly** ([Backbar Academy](https://academy.getbackbar.com/how-often-should-you-take-inventory-at-your-restaurant-or-bar)). A monthly counter may not count twice inside a 15-day trial that started at sign-up, so the first session can be their whole trial. In RevenueCat's Business category, 89.9% of trial starts happen on the install day ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2026-business)). That is a different kind of trial (an in-app paywall), but it points the same way: the first day decides it. Effort is not the problem in itself. People value what they built themselves only when the effort ends in a finished product ([Norton, Mochon & Ariely](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/12136084)). Setting pars and distributors announced as a one-time setup reads as an investment. The same work sprung on someone at 1 a.m., after a promise of "no organizing", reads as complication. It turns into abandonment if the first order never goes out.

Two more pieces of the "complicated" feeling come from outside the scan screens. First, **a fresh install opens on a returning-user screen**: "Welcome back / Sign in to keep your bar stocked". The only way to sign up by email, an outlined "Start Free Trial" button with a 25%-opacity border, sits **152 pt below the fold** on an iPhone 16 ([screenshot](<../research_notes/86d first run onboarding/screens/07-login-iphone16.png>)). On Register, "Create Account" is below the fold on both an iPhone 16 and an SE (271 pt and 383 pt hidden), and the fields set no autofill types or return-key chaining (`src/screens/RegisterScreen.tsx:166-220`). The white "Continue with Apple" button is the only filled control above the fold, so it is the obvious path, but nothing says it also creates an account. Second, **the warm sales conversation hands off to silence.** Registration sends no email (`86d-api/main.py:320-366`) and the app sends no push notifications. Apart from copies of any orders they send, the first message a new bar receives is the trial-ending reminder about five days before the end. It comes from "86'd Orders", is signed "The 86'd team" and has no reply-to (`main.py:3406-3460`), although the pitch said "call me directly at 910-335-2760". The bar-name screen asks for a phone number "for setup help", but nothing in code acts on it. A bar that has already signed up can still get cold follow-ups unless someone marks the lead `won` (`86d-api/crm.py:1519-1527, 4311-4326`).

Competitors show what fills that gap. Most positive "easy to set up" reviews name a person: Partender's Erwin "walked us through every step", and Bar Patrol's owner walked another reviewer through setup ([Partender reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/partender-bar-inventory/id726829071?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone); [Bar Patrol reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/bar-patrol-inventory-app/id1097511399?see-all=reviews&platform=ipad)). BevSpot wraps its free trial in "White Glove implementation" ([BevSpot](https://www.bevspot.com/pricing/)). Lixor is the closest self-serve rival: an iOS app with a no-card trial. In September and October 2026 it moved its permission prompts to the moment the user taps Start, and added a first-count walkthrough that uses the user's own bottle ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/lixor/id6747053861)). A self-serve app with no person behind it has to build that reassurance into the screens, or have the founder supply it by hand while volume is small.

At five sign-ups a week no change can be isolated anyway (see the last section), so batch what is clearly right and low-risk, write a dated changelog, and judge the build on observed first counts rather than funnel deltas. The order follows one argument. The biggest gap between expectation and experience comes before the app is even opened, in what the listing, website and pitch promise. That costs nothing to fix, and fixing it changes how every later screen feels. The founder hand-off is next because it is the one intervention every competitor's happiest reviewers credit, and at this volume one person can do it for every sign-up. Of the in-app changes, typeable par and the closed dead ends remove the only places where the first run can actually stall. The others reduce effort without unblocking anything. Letting people use the app before signing up was considered and set aside. Duolingo's reported ~20% lift from moving sign-up later is old, second-hand and from a consumer app ([Taplytics](https://taplytics.com/blog/duolingo-ab-test-onboarding)). Every 86'd scan costs two paid AI calls. And most arrivals come from the founder's calls, already motivated. A one-tap Apple path gets most of the same benefit.

### Past decisions: keep eleven, bend three

Most first-run choices are recorded with reasons, and the recommendations above keep those reasons intact. **Confirm Email stays.** Baymard's canonical advice is to "confirm their e-mail (rather than their password)" because a wrong address cannot be recovered ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/confirm-email-not-password)), and that matches the project's rule to keep it until verification exists. The cheaper protections are additions, not replacements. Autofill hints let iOS fill both fields with the real address. A domain-typo suggestion ("did you mean hotmail.com?") cut sign-up bounces by a self-reported 50% for Mailcheck's authors ([Mailcheck](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/mailcheck@1.1.1/README.md)). Making the Apple path obvious removes typing altogether. **Sign in with Apple, and not Google, stays.** Guideline 4.8 means adding Google would require an equivalent privacy-preserving login anyway, and no independent study isolates any login button's effect on conversion. **The explanation before the camera prompt stays.** Apple's onboarding guidance explicitly supports bringing a required permission into onboarding ([HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding)). A study of 772 smartphone users found requests with an explanation were approved more often ([Tan et al., CHI '14](https://blues.cs.berkeley.edu/?p=105)). No rigorous number exists for camera prompts specifically, so the project's claim that a pre-prompt "materially lifts opt-in" should be treated as directional. The escape from a denied camera stays exactly as built: the `canAskAgain` branch, Open Settings, the menu button, and the foreground re-read that calls `initScanning()`. So do the removal of the post-sign-up pitch screen, the bar-name step right after sign-up, par required with a minimum of 1, inline distributor creation with two fields, case-or-bottle decided for the bar, no delivery date, and no keyboard popping open on the login email. Moving "Start Free Trial" above the fold serves that last decision's own reason: new users need to see the create-account path. The unused `Onboarding.tsx` should not be brought back before sign-up either. Its copy ("detect bottles instantly", "Automatic par level comparison") overpromises, and the Vevo result argues against any carousel there.

Three things bend, and none of them reverses a recorded reason. The par stepper's "tap-only (no long-press repeat)" is a code comment with no reason given, and typing a number keeps par just as deliberate, which is what the no-skip rule protects. The start card as a mandatory tap before every count has no recorded reason at all; the code audit found none. The menu button on the start card exists because "without one a denied permission left no way out". Removing it only from the not-yet-asked version still satisfies that, because the denied screen keeps its menu.

## The camera pre-prompt invites an App Review rejection

Apple's privacy guidance allows a custom screen before a permission alert, with strict mechanics. It must "Include only one button", titled with "a term like 'Continue' or 'Next'". Using "Allow" on that button is named as manipulation. And it must not "provide a way for people to leave the screen or window without viewing the system alert — like offering an option to close or cancel" ([HIG Privacy](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/privacy)). This is enforced. In March 2026 a developer posted that a location pre-prompt with "Continue" and "Not Now" was rejected under 5.1.1(iv), with the reviewer writing: "The user should always proceed to the permission request after the message" ([Apple Developer Forums](https://origin-devforums.apple.com/forums/thread/817672)). 86'd has four exposures, one of them structural.

| Issue | What ships today | Rule it touches | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| A custom permission screen under the system alert | "Start Scanning" hides the card and calls `requestPermission()`. While access is undetermined, the render path draws "Camera Access Required / Enable camera access to scan bottles for inventory. / Enable Camera / Not now" (`CameraScan.tsx:253-265, 1017-1081`). On first run, that screen almost certainly sits behind iOS's own alert; this follows from the code and has not been seen on a device ([screenshot](<../research_notes/86d first run onboarding/screens/06a-permission-blocked-iphone16.png>)) | One button only, no cancel; "Enable" is close to "Allow"; the same layout as the March 2026 rejection | While access is undetermined, show a neutral placeholder with no buttons behind the alert. Keep "Enable Camera" out of that state |
| A way out of the pre-prompt card | The start card carries a menu button next to "Start Scanning" | "Don't provide a way for people to leave… without viewing the system alert" | On the version shown before access is asked, use one button labelled "Continue" and no menu. Put the reason first, at readable contrast: "Next, iOS asks to use your camera. 86'd only uses it to photograph the bottles you scan." The denied screen keeps its menu, so nobody is stranded |
| The shipped camera purpose string | Two strings sit in `app.json`. The expo-camera plugin's "Allow 86'd to access your camera to scan bottles." wins (confirmed with `npx expo config --type introspect`). It opens with "Allow" and leaves out barcodes, which the app also reads | 5.1.1(ii): purpose strings must "clearly and completely describe your use" ([Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)); Apple's model is a specific, active sentence ([Tech Talk](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/tech-talks/110152)) | Put the specific sentence in the plugin's `cameraPermission`, e.g. "86'd uses the camera to photograph a bottle's label so it can name the bottle, and to read barcodes." |
| An inaccurate photo-library string | `NSPhotoLibraryUsageDescription` says the app "saves photos of bottles for inventory records". Scan photos stay in the app's own folder; the library is only read by Manual Add's picker | 5.1.1(ii) accuracy | Describe the picker: "Choose a photo of a bottle to add it." |

Two related items carry less risk. Guideline 5.1.1(iv) also asks apps to "provide alternative solutions for users who don't grant consent", and the denied screen could offer "Add bottles by name instead" through Manual Add. And the listing's Support URL opens the privacy policy, which sits uneasily with guideline 1.5's requirement for "an easy way to contact you". The current permission design appears to have shipped already: #26's changes landed on 2026-09-24 and 1.0.5 went live on 2026-10-09 ([iTunes lookup](https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?id=6798359825&country=us)). So at least one reviewer let it through. That lowers the odds of a rejection without removing the exposure, because reviewers differ. A rejection would cost a review cycle and a $2–4 rebuild, not users. The fixes also help users: today, someone tapping "Start Scanning" for the first time sees two "please allow" messages stacked on each other.

## Five sign-ups a week need timelines, not A/B tests

At about five sign-ups a week, percentages cannot tell versions apart. Two versions with 10 users each, at 3/10 against 6/10, give Fisher p ≈ 0.37. Only a jump like 2/10 to 8/10 reaches p ≈ 0.02. Detecting 30% → 45% with 80% power needs about 160 users per version, roughly 64 weeks. Three of five sign-ups activating has a 95% interval of **23%–88%** (adjusted-Wald method, [MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/wald/)). And zero failures among five users still allows a true failure rate of up to 45%. These calculations come from the research notes. In practice: change one cluster of things, date it, read every account's story, and only believe large effects.

The first job is to make the numbers mean what they say. The CRM's "activation" counts users with any `inventory_sessions` row. Today's app writes one only when it calls `POST /orders/email` (`86d-api/main.py:3301-3358`). So **"completed a count" actually means "tried to send an order"**. A bar that scanned 60 bottles and stalled at pars looks the same as a bar that never opened the camera. The in-app funnel also misses every Apple sign-up, because the Apple path fires no event (`src/components/AppleSignInButton.tsx:39-80`), although `users.auth_provider` records it server-side. Most of the timeline can be built today, with no app release, from what the server already logs. Missing pieces need fixed-name events, which keeps the backend's no-free-form-properties design intact.

| Step | Already on the server | New fixed-name client event |
|---|---|---|
| Sign-up method | `users.created_at`, `auth_provider` | `apple_signup_succeeded` |
| Bar name | `users.business_name`, `phone` | `bar_name_skipped` |
| Start card and camera | — | `start_screen_viewed`, `start_scanning_tapped`, `camera_granted`, `camera_denied`, `camera_settings_opened`, `camera_recovered` |
| First scan | `scan_events` (time, status, match method, latency), `scan_outcomes` | none needed |
| First bottle saved | `inventory_drafts.updated_at` | `first_bottle_saved` |
| Review | `par_levels.par_set_at` | `review_opened`, `par_gate_hit` |
| Distributor | `distributors.created_at` | `distributor_sheet_opened`, `distributor_added` |
| Order | `orders.order_number`, time | `all_stocked_shown`, `order_send_tapped` |
| Paywall | `subscription_status` | `paywall_viewed` |

Define three milestones and read them per user, tagged with the lead source from CRM attribution. A **setup moment**: the first bottles saved in session one. A **value moment**: the first order emailed within seven days. A **habit moment**: a second count within 14 days. Lenny Rachitsky's activation survey suggests a good milestone retains users at least twice as well as missing it, and the typical time-bound window is 7–10 days ([Lenny](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-activation-rate)). Revisit the definitions at 50–100 sign-ups. Until then, spend 30 minutes a week on a one-line timeline for every new account. For example: "Apple, 11:48 pm → first scan +3 min → 23 bottles → par gate hit → no distributor → trial day 6". Add what the founder knows from the call. Before adding events, check the App Privacy label. It declares only Diagnostics under analytics. Product-interaction events tied to a signed-in user may also need declaring, and 5.1.1(ii) asks for consent "even if such data is considered to be anonymous". This is a compliance question to check, not legal advice. Avoid session-replay SDKs. Apple made apps remove or disclose screen recording in 2019 ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/news/apple-cracks-down-on-apps-that-record-your-screen)), and recording would capture the camera view and the bar's stock.

The highest-value measurement is watching. With permission, join each new bar's first count over FaceTime or in person. That is also the founder hand-off from change 2, so measuring it costs no extra time. Separately, run a five-person think-aloud on a preview build with bartenders who are not customers. Use a real, dimly lit shelf, ten or more bottles, and a test order sent to your own address. Run two rounds, because any single group of five has found as little as 55% of the problems ([Faulkner 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14587545/); [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-many-test-users/)). Watch for these things:

- **Getting in:** the minutes from install to the first saved bottle, and whether people find "Start Free Trial" or simply take Apple.
- **Framing:** how far from the shelf they stand, which bottle they center, and whether light or glare causes a retake.
- **The pad:** whether they wait for the name or tap Add early, and how they enter an open bottle.
- **Review:** what their face does when it opens. Ask them to say what "Unassigned" and "PAR" mean, and count the taps they spend on pars.
- **Distributors:** whether they have each rep's order email at hand at 1 a.m. If they don't, letting them assign a distributor by name in Review and add its email at send time is the next thing to try.
- **The first order:** whether it goes out in the same session.

Three to seven days later, ask a new bar about what they did, not what they think: "Walk me through your last count", "What almost stopped you?" At trial end, ask anyone who did not subscribe what decided it. Rare failures, such as a denied camera or a dead-zone scan, will not show up in five sessions, so the logs have to catch them.

## Conclusion

The owner framed the problem as teaching: too much explained, or too little. The evidence says it is about expectations and setup. 86'd's real advantage over its category is that the first count builds the product list. Competitors charge setup fees for that, or call it the most time-consuming step. But the marketing hides this advantage by denying there is any setup at all, so the remaining setup (par and distributor, once per bottle) arrives as a surprise. The same work, announced honestly and made cheap to do, becomes the story that sells the second count: set it once, and next week only the numbers are asked.

At this scale, the founder is both the best onboarding system 86'd has and its best measurement tool. Every competitor review that calls setup easy names a person. And a single observed first count will show more about where bars stall than months of funnel percentages at five sign-ups a week. The start card the owner was worried about turns out to be the least important part of the first run, and the cheapest to fix.
